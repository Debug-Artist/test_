Derivation of the Mean Squared Error Gradient

Let our training set comprise $m$ observations of $n=5$ standardized agronomic features. The linear hypothesis function is formulated as:

$$\hat{y}^{(i)} = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}^{(i)} + b = \sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_{ij} + b$$

The discrepancy between predicted yields $\hat{y}$ and actual harvest measurements $y$ is quantified by the Mean Squared Error (MSE) objective function:

$$L(\mathbf{w}, b) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left( \hat{y}^{(i)} - y^{(i)} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \left( \sum_{j=1}^n w_j x_{ij} + b - y_i \right)^2$$

Step-by-Step Multivariable Chain Rule

To minimize $L$, we compute the sensitivity of the loss with respect to each individual weight $w_j$ by treating all other parameters as invariant constants. Let the scalar residual be $e_i = (\hat{y}_i - y_i)$:

STEP 1
Differentiate the Outer Quadratic Loss: $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial e_i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial e_i} \left[ \frac{1}{m} e_i^2 \right] = \frac{2}{m} e_i = \frac{2}{m} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)$$
STEP 2
Differentiate the Inner Hypothesis with respect to $w_j$: $$\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial w_j} = \frac{\partial}{\partial w_j} \left( w_1 x_{i1} + \dots + w_j x_{ij} + \dots + w_n x_{in} + b - y_i \right) = x_{ij}$$
STEP 3
Combine via Chain Rule for Analytical Weight Gradients: $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j} = \sum_{i=1}^m \frac{\partial L}{\partial e_i} \frac{\partial e_i}{\partial w_j} = \mathbf{\frac{2}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m (\hat{y}_i - y_i) x_{ij}}$$
STEP 4
Analytical Partial Derivative with respect to Bias $b$: $$\frac{\partial e_i}{\partial b} = 1 \implies \mathbf{\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = \frac{2}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m (\hat{y}_i - y_i)}$$

Pure NumPy Vectorization

Rather than iterating sample-by-sample via slow Python loops, the backend evaluates the gradient across the entire design matrix $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times 5}$ in parallel:

The Project's Exact Momentum Formulation

In real-world agricultural datasets, differing feature scales (e.g., rainfall $1000\text{ mm}$ vs. soil pH $6.5$) distort the loss surface into an elongated elliptical ravine. Ordinary gradient descent oscillates violently across the steep walls.

Our platform implements the exact momentum recurrence relation specified in your project thesis:

$$\mathbf{V}_t = \mathbf{V}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta) \nabla L(\theta_{t-1})$$ $$\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \eta \mathbf{V}_t$$

Where:

  • $\mathbf{V}_t$: Accumulated parameter velocity vector at epoch $t$, initialized to $\mathbf{0}$.
  • $\beta \in [0, 1)$: Momentum damping factor ($0.90$).
  • $(1 - \beta)$: Scale factor applied to the newly computed gradient step.
  • $\eta$: Learning rate ($0.01$).
  • $\theta_t$: Parameter vector $[\mathbf{w}^T, b]^T$.

Comparative Analysis of Momentum Conventions

Optimization Variant Velocity Recurrence Physical Interpretation
Your Project Convention $V_t = V_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\nabla L$ Digital Integrator: Retains 100% of historical velocity and adds scaled incremental gradients.
Polyak's Heavy-Ball $V_t = \beta V_{t-1} + \nabla L$ Exponential decay on velocity with unit-weighted incoming gradient.
PyTorch / EMA Momentum $V_t = \beta V_{t-1} + (1-\beta)\nabla L$ Exponentially Weighted Moving Average of gradients.

Why Velocity Dampens Ravines

Consider two successive steps across a valley wall:

$$\nabla L_1 = \begin{bmatrix} +15.0 \\ +0.2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \nabla L_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -14.8 \\ +0.2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Lateral updates cancel out:

$$+15.0 + (-14.8) = +0.2 \approx 0$$

Longitudinal updates accumulate:

$$+0.2 + (+0.2) = +0.4 \text{ (Forward Boost)}$$

Taylor Series Expansion & Local Linearization

To demonstrate why gradient descent moves opposite to the gradient, consider the second-order multivariable Taylor series expansion around parameter vector $\theta$:

$$L(\theta + \Delta \theta) \approx L(\theta) + \nabla L(\theta)^T \Delta \theta + \frac{1}{2} \Delta \theta^T \mathbf{H} \Delta \theta$$

Where $\mathbf{H} = \nabla^2 L(\theta) = \frac{2}{m}\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X}$ is the Hessian Matrix of second-order partial derivatives.

First-Order Linearization

For an infinitesimal step size $\|\Delta \theta\| \to 0$, the quadratic curvature term becomes negligible. The change in loss reduces to:

$$\Delta L = L(\theta + \Delta \theta) - L(\theta) \approx \nabla L(\theta)^T \Delta \theta$$

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the scalar inner product $\nabla L^T \Delta \theta$ achieves its maximum negative value when the displacement vector $\Delta \theta$ is oriented strictly antiparallel to the gradient vector:

$$\Delta \theta = -\eta \nabla L(\theta) \implies \Delta L \approx -\eta \|\nabla L(\theta)\|^2 < 0$$

This proves that gradient descent is mathematically guaranteed to decrease loss locally.

Numerical Stability Bound

For the step to remain stable and avoid exploding gradients ($L \to \infty$), the learning rate must be strictly bounded by the maximum eigenvalue of the Hessian matrix:

$$\eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{H})} = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{2}{m}\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\right)}$$

Feature scaling ensures $\lambda_{\max} \approx 2$, making $\eta = 0.01$ robustly stable.
